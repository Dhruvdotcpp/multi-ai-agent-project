**Task:** Evaluate whether multi-agent AI systems are worth using compared to a single LLM

## Summary  
The sources reviewed show that multi-agent AI systems can achieve higher accuracy than single LLMs on a range of benchmarks, but they also incur substantial coordination overhead, higher computational cost, and frequent failures in production. Empirical evaluations report that even top-performing multi-agent models complete only about half of their assigned tasks, and coordination-related breakdowns account for roughly 37 % of errors, with overall failure rates ranging from 41 % to 86.7 % when coordination is poorly managed. While multi-agent architectures provide fault tolerance and modular extensibility that may reduce long-term development effort for heterogeneous or complex workflows, these benefits are often offset by latency, resource consumption, and the risk of cascading failures. For simpler, sequential workloads, a single, more capable LLM typically offers lower compute cost, easier governance, and more predictable performance, making it the more practical choice.

## Findings  

### Accuracy and task performance  
Side-by-side empirical comparisons on benchmarks such as GPQA-Diamond, IFEval, and MuSR show that multi-agent systems (MAS) consistently outperform single LLMs on accuracy across all tested benchmarks [4]. However, the top-performing multi-agent model still completes only about half of its assigned tasks, indicating that higher accuracy does not translate into complete task success [2].

### Coordination overhead and resource use  
Multi-agent setups require agents to exchange messages or tool calls, and this coordination introduces latency and resource consumption that grow with the number of agents [12]. The Nature Machine Intelligence article defines coordination overhead as a percentage increase in runtime and notes that message density (inter-agent messages per reasoning turn) can be substantial [7]. Scaling such systems incurs an exponential "coordination tax," where context loss, memory silos, and synchronization overhead can outweigh marginal intelligence gains [9]. Consequently, multi-agent systems demand more computation and incur higher inference latency and operational costs than single-agent systems [13].

### Failure modes and production reliability  
Failure modes unique to multi-agent configurations—cascading failures, deadlocks, and "memory poisoning"—are absent in single-agent architectures [6, 8]. Coordination failures alone constitute roughly 37 % of breakdowns in multi-agent LLM systems [6], and reported production failure rates range from 41 % to 86.7 % when coordination is poorly managed [6, 8]. The primary sources of these breakdowns are specification ambiguity and unstructured coordination protocols, which lead to role misinterpretation, duplicated work, or skipped verification [6].

### Benefits of modularity and fault tolerance  
Multi-agent architectures break complex tasks into subtasks assigned to specialized agents that operate autonomously with their own tools, memory, and reasoning abilities, coordinated by an overarching framework [11]. This modularity can provide fault tolerance—failure of one agent does not halt the entire workflow—and may lower long-term development costs for heterogeneous tasks [10]. Frameworks such as SoundHound's Agentic+ further illustrate how deterministic orchestration can assign each subtask to the most efficient model, blending generative AI with multi-agent coordination [14].

### Suitability for different workload types  
For simple, sequential workloads, a single, more capable LLM avoids coordination overhead, is more resource-efficient, and is easier to govern, test, and predict, making it the preferred solution [10]. In contrast, multi-agent systems may be justified for complex, heterogeneous tasks where modular extensibility and fault tolerance outweigh the added coordination costs and higher failure risk.
